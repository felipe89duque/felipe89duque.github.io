Equilibrium Potentials & the GHK Equation

Part 1: Ion Concentration Gradients

The cell membrane separates two very different ionic environments:

Typical resting ion concentrations in vertebrate neurons
Ion Extracellular (mM) Intracellular (mM) Ratio (out/in)
K⁺ (Potassium) ~5 ~140 1:28 (low outside)
Na⁺ (Sodium) ~145 ~12 12:1 (high outside)
Cl⁻ (Chloride) ~110 ~4 27:1 (high outside)

Key insight: These gradients are maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, which uses energy to push Na⁺ out and K⁺ in.

Part 2: Equilibrium Potentials (The Nernst Equation)

If the membrane were permeable to only one ion, that ion would flow down its concentration gradient until an electrical gradient built up to oppose it. The voltage at which this happens is called the equilibrium potential.

Eion = (61.5 mV / z) × log₁₀([ion]outside / [ion]inside)

Where z = charge of the ion (+1 for K⁺ and Na⁺, -1 for Cl⁻)

Example: Equilibrium Potential for K⁺

EK = (61.5 / 1) × log₁₀(5 / 140)

EK = 61.5 × log₁₀(0.036)

EK = 61.5 × (-1.44)

EK ≈ -89 mV

This means that if the membrane were permeable only to K⁺, the membrane potential would settle at -89 mV.

Physiological Equilibrium Potentials

K⁺
≈ -89 mV

Negative because K⁺ is more concentrated inside

Na⁺
≈ +60 mV

Positive because Na⁺ is more concentrated outside

Cl⁻
≈ -70 mV

Negative because Cl⁻ is more concentrated outside

Key insight: The actual resting membrane potential (~-70 mV) is between EK and ENa because the membrane is permeable to both ions (though more to K⁺).

Part 3: The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) Equation

Real cells are permeable to multiple ions at the same time. The GHK equation describes the membrane potential when several ions are present and the membrane is permeable to all of them:

Vm = (61.5 mV) × log₁₀ [ (PK[K⁺]out + PNa[Na⁺]out + PCl[Cl⁻]in) / (PK[K⁺]in + PNa[Na⁺]in + PCl[Cl⁻]out) ]

Pion = permeability to that ion (0 to 1)
